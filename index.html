Test 2: Medical interpretation

Format (Confirmed): same tool. Each question video is something you must interpret, and you record your interpretation.

Content (Likely): short segments of a doctor–patient conversation. Arabic patient lines → you say them in English; possibly English doctor lines → you say them in Arabic. Possibly a list of medical terms or a short passage. Expect Egyptian or Levantine dialect.

How to answer:

  • Notes during the video: numbers, doses, times, negatives.
  • When REC appears, interpret immediately, first person ("My chest hurts", never "she says").
  • Interpret everything: no summary, no explaining.
  • Small slip? Fix it out loud: "Interpreter correction: …" and continue.
  • If the video asks you to introduce yourself as the interpreter first, use your script from the Scripts tab.
Smart use of "Repeat"Repeat is your only way to hear a segment twice, and you have very few (likely 3 for the whole test). Save them for the interpretation test, for a segment where you missed a number or a whole sentence. Decide fast: press Repeat in the first seconds, not after a long answer. Don't use it for a small slip. Use "Interpreter correction" instead.

The 12 rules that pass the test

These come from the US national standards (NCIHC Code of Ethics and Standards of Practice), which every American healthcare interpreting company follows. Raters listen for them.

  1. Interpret everything.No adding, no leaving out, no summarizing. Repeat it even if it's rude, repeated, or seems unimportant.
  2. Always first person.Doctor: "Do you have a fever?" You: "هل عندك حرارة؟" Never "He's asking if…". Patient: "My chest hurts", never "She says her chest hurts".
  3. Keep the tone and level.Formal stays formal, angry stays angry, medical words stay medical. Don't simplify the doctor's words yourself. If the patient doesn't understand, they ask the doctor.
  4. When you speak for yourself, say "the interpreter"."The interpreter needs a repetition." Then tell the other side what you just asked. This is called transparency.
  5. Never give advice or your opinion.If the patient asks you "What should I do?", you interpret the question to the doctor. You don't answer it.
  6. Never lose numbers, negatives, or time.Doses, dates, "not", "only", "for three days". Write numbers down the moment you hear them.
  7. Don't convert or explain.Don't turn ounces into cups or Celsius into Fahrenheit. Converting is adding.
  8. Correct yourself right away."Interpreter correction: the doctor said fifty milligrams, not fifteen."
  9. Manage the flow politely.If someone talks too long: "Excuse me, the interpreter needs to interpret now." Then continue.
  10. Confidentiality (HIPAA).Nothing from a call leaves the call. Destroy notes after every call. Never record.
  11. Stay neutral.No side-chats with the patient. If you know the patient personally, tell the company and step out.
  12. Safety first.Anything about suicide, abuse, chest pain, or allergy is interpreted immediately and completely, even if the patient says "don't tell the doctor".

The NCIHC Code of Ethics, 9 principles (memorize the names)

#PrincipleIn one line
1ConfidentialityEverything stays inside the care team.
2AccuracyFull message and its spirit, nothing added or omitted.
3ImpartialityNo opinions, no bias, no advice.
4RespectTreat every party with dignity.
5Cultural awarenessKnow both cultures, including US hospital culture.
6Role boundariesInterpret only. No personal involvement.
7ProfessionalismOn time, prepared, honest about limits.
8Professional developmentKeep learning terms and skills.
9AdvocacyRare. Only when the patient's safety or dignity is at serious risk and other steps failed.

Least-intrusive rule: first just interpret. If needed, clarify a word. If needed, flag a possible cultural misunderstanding. Advocacy is last.

Tricky situations and what to do

SituationWhat you do
Bad audio, can't hear"The interpreter cannot hear clearly. Could you please repeat?" Never guess. Tell the other party what you asked.
Speaker talks for a long timePolitely interrupt: "Excuse me, the interpreter needs to interpret now." Interpret, then ask them to pause after short phrases.
Unknown medical word"The interpreter requests clarification of the term ___." Tell the patient you asked. Drug names stay in English.
Patient asks your opinionInterpret the question to the doctor. If it's directed at you: "The patient asked the interpreter for an opinion. As the interpreter I can't give advice," then interpret it to the doctor.
"Don't tell the doctor…"Remind them everything is interpreted, then interpret it, including the "don't tell" part.
Family members talk to each otherInterpret it. Side conversations are part of the session.
Patient mentions suicide or self-harmInterpret immediately, word for word. Never soften. The clinician decides what happens next.
Dialect you don't understandBe honest: "The interpreter is having difficulty with the patient's dialect." Ask for repetition or a different interpreter. Never pretend.

The traps

  • 15 vs 50, 13 vs 30, 14 vs 40. On the phone they sound alike. If not 100% sure, ask: "The interpreter requests a repetition of the number."
  • Always write the unit with the number: 500 mg, 10 units, 2 puffs.
  • Frequency + duration: "twice a day for 10 days." Don't drop the second half.
  • Don't convert Celsius↔Fahrenheit, kg↔lb, ounces↔cups.
  • Read blood pressure as "148 over 95" / ١٤٨ على ٩٥.
  • Money: "$4.15" = four dollars and fifteen cents / أربعة دولارات وخمسة عشر سنتًا.

Prepared answers (60–90 seconds each)

Practice these aloud and record them. Change the wording so it sounds like you.

Tell us about yourself

"My name is Safa Mohamed Ahmed, and I'm from Alexandria, Egypt. I graduated from the Faculty of Law at Alexandria University, English Section, in 2025, and I'm now completing a diploma in Public Law. Studying law in English trained me to be precise with language, to handle sensitive information confidentially, and to stay neutral. Arabic is my native language, and I've been interpreting between Arabic and English for people around me. I want to build that into a professional career in medical interpreting, where accuracy really matters."

Why do you want to be a medical interpreter?

"Because language should never be the reason a patient doesn't get the right care. When a patient can't explain their symptoms or understand their medication, mistakes happen. My legal background taught me how much every word matters, and I'd like to use that precision to help patients and doctors understand each other completely."

Tell us about your interpreting experience
Keep this answer trueOnly describe situations that really happened. Training, QA call reviews, and the ALTA test will show your real level within days. A real example plus "I've been training intensively" is stronger than invented details you'd have to defend later.

"Most of my interpreting so far has been informal: helping family members and other people communicate in English, for example [a real example: a clinic visit, a pharmacy, a phone call with a company, a foreign visitor]. That taught me to listen carefully, keep the full meaning, and stay calm. To prepare for medical work, I've been studying medical terminology in both languages, the NCIHC code of ethics and standards of practice, and practicing doctor–patient role-plays, recording myself and checking my accuracy."

Describe a difficult situation and how you handled it (STAR)

Situation: what was happening. Task: what you had to do. Action: what you did, step by step. Result: how it ended and what you learned. Pick a real moment from university, an exam, a stressful family situation, or interpreting for someone. End with what you learned: "I learned to stay calm and ask for clarification instead of guessing."

What would you do if you didn't understand a word during a call?

"I would never guess. I would say, 'The interpreter requests clarification,' ask the speaker to explain or repeat, and then tell the other party what I asked, so everything stays transparent."

How do you handle stress and long shifts?

"I prepare my workspace before the shift, stay focused on one call at a time, and take my scheduled breaks properly. During a hard call I slow down, take notes, and follow the protocol. After the call, I let it go and get ready for the next one."
